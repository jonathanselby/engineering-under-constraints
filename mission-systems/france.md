# France — Mission-Specific Simplification

Sources: [FR-1], [FR-2] in the [source register](../sources/bibliography.md).

## Problem

Aircraft programmes often accumulate capability because every additional sensor or subsystem appears individually valuable. The Mirage 5 is useful as a counter-example: Dassault deliberately simplified the Mirage III E design for a narrower low-altitude tactical-support role.

## Constraints

- mission emphasis on low-altitude attack rather than all-weather interception;
- cost;
- fuel/range;
- payload;
- available volume and weight.

## Engineering response

Dassault records that the Mirage 5 removed the radar, used the nose volume for navigation/attack equipment, increased fuel capacity and simplified the layout. [FR-1]

This was not simplification for its own sake. Capability was removed to improve fit against a narrower mission.

## What was exceptional?

The design demonstrates that a derivative can become more useful for a specific role by doing less. The result was cheaper, carried more fuel than the Mirage III C and became Dassault's most widely exported combat aircraft. [FR-1]

Export success is not proof that every trade-off was technically superior, but it is evidence that the simplified configuration met a broad set of customer needs.

## Limits

- removing radar reduced capability outside the intended mission;
- simplification only works when the mission is genuinely bounded;
- manufacturer history is not independent evaluation;
- a design optimised for one operating environment can become fragile when requirements change.

## Transferable software lesson

> **Removing capability can be an architectural improvement when the mission boundary is real.**

The correct question is not “what else can we add?” but “which capabilities materially help this system perform the job it exists to do?”
